> **What is Deep Maps?** An interactive geospatial storytelling platform. Users explore curated historical moments tied to real places on a map. Each "moment" is a specific event at a specific lat/lng — verb-first naming. We have 602 moments, 195 stories, 210 entities, and 30 collections.

## Audit Checklist

For each moment/story/entity you review, check:

1. **Five-Second Test**: Can someone who has never used the app understand what happened here within 5 seconds of reading the moment name + subtitle?
2. **Verb-First Event Name**: Is the moment name an event description, not a place name or noun phrase? Pattern: "[Subject] [Past-Tense Verb] [Object/Detail]"
3. **Subtitle adds value**: Does the subtitle provide the "second hook" (irony, stakes, human detail) WITHOUT restating the name?
4. **Description is standalone**: Does the description work as a mini-article without requiring story context?
5. **Past tense consistency**: Are events described in past tense? Flag present tense ("Shoots" should be "Shot").
6. **Neutral encyclopedic tone**: Wikipedia-style matter-of-fact clarity. Not preachy, sensational, or devotional.
7. **Factual accuracy**: Anything wrong, anachronistic, or unverifiable?
8. **Entity descriptions**: Do they frontload the hook in the first 8 words? (No "Born [real name]" openings.)

### Content Format Reference

Each moment below is formatted as:

```
[notability score] · **[Moment Name]** (year)
*[Subtitle]*
[Description]
Kind: [kind] | Entities: [entity IDs]
```

- **Notability score**: 0-100. Higher = more globally significant. 80+ are world-zoom moments.
- **Kind**: event (default), presence (ongoing location), death, birth, creation, discovery
- Moment names should be verb-first past tense events with specific subjects.

### Literary Titans
**ID**: `literary-titans` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1599–1973 | **Moments**: 7

> Shakespeare building the Globe, Dostoevsky surviving a mock execution, Tolstoy dying in a train station, García Márquez pawning his car to finish a masterpiece, Kafka writing through the night, and Tagore winning Asia's first Nobel — the writers who defined their civilizations.

**84** · **Shakespeare Opens the Globe Theatre on the South Bank of the Thames** (1599)
*A glove-maker's son from Stratford builds the most famous playhouse in history*
In 1599, William Shakespeare and his company dismantled their old theatre north of the Thames (after a lease dispute), carried the timbers across the river, and rebuilt them as the Globe Theatre in Southwark. Over the next 14 years, Shakespeare premiered his greatest works: Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, and The Tempest. In 1613, a cannon set the thatched roof ablaze during Henry VIII. Shakespeare retired to Stratford-upon-Avon and died in 1616.
Kind: event | Entities: william-shakespeare

**39** · **Dostoevsky Faces a Firing Squad — Then Is Told It Was a Hoax** (1849)
*A mock execution transforms a young writer into literature's greatest explorer of human suffering*
On December 22, 1849, Fyodor Dostoevsky and 20 other political prisoners were led to Semyonov Square in St. Petersburg, bound to stakes, and blindfolded before a firing squad. The soldiers raised their rifles. At the last moment, a messenger arrived with a "reprieve" — the entire execution had been staged as psychological torture. One prisoner went permanently insane on the spot. Dostoevsky was sentenced to four years of hard labor in Siberia. The experience shaped Crime and Punishment, The Brothers Karamazov, and The Idiot.
Kind: event | Entities: fyodor-dostoevsky

**39** · **Tolstoy Abandons His Estate at 82 and Dies in a Train Station** (1910)
*The author of War and Peace walks out on his family and fortune — and dies ten days later*
On October 28, 1910, 82-year-old Leo Tolstoy left his estate at Yasnaya Polyana in the middle of the night. He had spent decades torn between his aristocratic life and his conviction that private property was immoral. After ten days of confused travel, he developed pneumonia and was carried off at Astapovo station, where he died on November 7 in the stationmaster's house. His death made worldwide news. He was buried at Yasnaya Polyana with no religious ceremony, as he wished.
Kind: event | Entities: leo-tolstoy

**37** · **García Márquez Writes One Hundred Years of Solitude in 18 Months of Self-Imposed Poverty** (1967)
*A journalist pawns his car and heater to finish a novel that will sell 50 million copies*
In 1965, while driving from Mexico City to Acapulco, Gabriel García Márquez was struck by a sudden vision of the novel he had been trying to write for 15 years. He turned the car around and told his wife Mercedes he needed 18 months. She pawned their car, their heater, and a food mixer, and kept the household running on credit while he wrote every day. The result — One Hundred Years of Solitude — sold out its first print run in a week and has since sold over 50 million copies. It won him the Nobel Prize in 1982.
Kind: event | Entities: gabriel-garcia-marquez

**35** · **Pablo Neruda Dies Twelve Days After Pinochet's Coup Under Disputed Circumstances** (1973)
*Chile's greatest poet lies dying while soldiers burn books in the streets*
On September 23, 1973, twelve days after Pinochet's military coup, Pablo Neruda died at a Santiago clinic. The official cause was cancer, but in 2023, forensic experts concluded he did not die of cancer and found a toxic bacterium in his remains. Neruda had been Chile's most beloved poet, a senator, a communist, and the 1971 Nobel laureate. His houses were ransacked by the military. At his funeral, mourners chanted his poetry through the streets — one of the first public acts of resistance against the dictatorship.
Kind: event | Entities: pablo-neruda

**39** · **Kafka Writes The Trial While Working Full-Time at an Insurance Company in Prague** (1914)
*A man who burned 90% of his work becomes the most influential novelist of the 20th century*
In 1914, Franz Kafka began writing The Trial — about a man arrested for an unspecified crime — while working as a senior clerk at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute in Prague. He wrote at night, often from 11 PM to 6 AM, then went to work. He never finished and asked his friend Max Brod to burn all his manuscripts. Brod refused. The Trial, The Castle, and Amerika were published posthumously and became foundational texts of modern literature. The word "Kafkaesque" entered every major language.
Kind: event | Entities: franz-kafka

**39** · **Rabindranath Tagore Wins the Nobel Prize — the First Non-European Laureate** (1913)
*A Bengali poet who wrote the national anthems of two countries changes how the West sees Asia*
In 1913, Rabindranath Tagore won the Nobel Prize in Literature for Gitanjali, becoming the first non-European laureate. Tagore was a poet, novelist, playwright, painter, and composer who wrote roughly 2,230 songs, including the national anthems of both India and Bangladesh. He founded Visva-Bharati University at Santiniketan as an alternative to colonial education, teaching classes outdoors under trees. He renounced his British knighthood after the 1919 Amritsar massacre.
Kind: milestone | Entities: rabindranath-tagore

### London's Great Stages
**ID**: `london-great-stages` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1599–1969 | **Moments**: 5

> Newton's laws of the universe, the key to Egyptian hieroglyphics, a glass cathedral of human achievement, and the Beatles' final performance — the moments when London was the stage for ideas that changed everything.

**84** · **Shakespeare Opens the Globe Theatre on the South Bank of the Thames** (1599)
*A glove-maker's son from Stratford builds the most famous playhouse in history*
In 1599, William Shakespeare and his company dismantled their old theatre north of the Thames (after a lease dispute), carried the timbers across the river, and rebuilt them as the Globe Theatre in Southwark. Over the next 14 years, Shakespeare premiered his greatest works: Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth, and The Tempest. In 1613, a cannon set the thatched roof ablaze during Henry VIII. Shakespeare retired to Stratford-upon-Avon and died in 1616.
Kind: event | Entities: william-shakespeare

**41** · **Isaac Newton Publishes the Principia and Rewrites the Laws of the Universe** (1687)
*A reclusive Cambridge professor explains gravity, motion, and the orbits of planets in one book*
On July 5, 1687, Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica was published in London, funded by astronomer Edmond Halley after the Royal Society ran out of money. In three volumes, Newton laid out the laws of motion and universal gravitation, explaining everything from falling apples to planetary orbits with a single mathematical framework. The Principia is widely regarded as the most important scientific work ever published. Newton was 44 and had developed the ideas twenty years earlier but never bothered to publish them until Halley coaxed him.
Kind: event | Entities: isaac-newton

**40** · **The Rosetta Stone Arrives at the British Museum After Britain Defeats Napoleon in Egypt** (1802)
*A slab of granodiorite becomes the key to deciphering 3,000 years of Egyptian hieroglyphics*
In 1802, the Rosetta Stone arrived at the British Museum as spoils of war after the French surrender in Egypt. The stone, discovered by Napoleon's soldiers in 1799 near the town of Rashid (Rosetta), bore the same decree in three scripts: hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek. It took another twenty years before Jean-François Champollion used the stone to crack the hieroglyphic code in 1822, unlocking 3,000 years of Egyptian history. The Rosetta Stone remains the British Museum's most visited object and Egypt's most requested repatriation.
Kind: event | Entities: napoleon-bonaparte

**39** · **Six Million Visitors Pour into the Crystal Palace for the Great Exhibition** (1851)
*A glass cathedral in Hyde Park showcases the peak of industrial civilization*
On May 1, 1851, Queen Victoria opened the Great Exhibition in Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace — a revolutionary iron-and-glass structure covering 19 acres of Hyde Park. Over 140 days, six million people (a third of Britain's population) visited 100,000 exhibits from around the world, from the Koh-i-Noor diamond to early voting machines. The exhibition turned a profit of £186,000, which Prince Albert used to found the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum, and the Natural History Museum. The Crystal Palace was later moved to Sydenham Hill, where it burned down in 1936.
Kind: event | Entities: queen-victoria

**41** · **The Beatles Play Their Final Concert on a London Rooftop** (1969)
*Four musicians climb to a rooftop on Savile Row and play together for the last time*
On January 30, 1969, the Beatles climbed to the roof of their Apple Corps headquarters at 3 Savile Row and played an unannounced 42-minute concert that stopped traffic across central London. It was the band's first live performance in over two years and their last ever. Office workers watched from neighboring rooftops, crowds gathered in the street below, and the Metropolitan Police eventually arrived to shut it down. John Lennon's closing words — "I'd like to say thank you on behalf of the group and ourselves and I hope we passed the audition" — became one of rock's most famous sign-offs.
Kind: event | Entities: the-beatles

### The Holy Sites of Jerusalem
**ID**: `jerusalem-holy-sites` | **Type**: place | **Years**: 19 BCE–present | **Moments**: 5

> The square kilometer of ancient stone where three world religions converge — the holiest sites of Christianity, the last wall of the Jewish Temple, and the rock from which Muhammad ascended to heaven, all within walking distance of each other.

**83** · **Constantine Builds a Church Over the Site of the Crucifixion and Resurrection** (335)
*Where Christianity's holiest site has stood since 335 CE, enclosing both Golgotha and the empty tomb*
The Church of the Holy Sepulchre encompasses both Golgotha (Calvary), where Jesus was crucified, and the tomb where he was buried and resurrected — the holiest site in Christianity. Commissioned by Emperor Constantine in 326 CE after his mother Helena identified the locations, the church was consecrated in 335 CE. Destroyed by Caliph al-Hakim in 1009, rebuilt by the Crusaders, and shared today by six Christian denominations under an uneasy arrangement codified in 1853.
 | Entities: jesus

**37** · **Herod the Great Builds the Retaining Wall That Becomes Judaism's Holiest Prayer Site** (-19)
*Where the last surviving wall of the Second Temple has drawn Jewish prayers for two millennia*
The Western Wall is the last remaining retaining wall of the Second Temple complex, built by Herod the Great around 19 BCE as part of a massive expansion of the Temple Mount platform. After the Roman destruction of the Temple in 70 CE, the wall became the closest accessible point to the Holy of Holies and the holiest site where Jews are permitted to pray. The lower seven courses of massive stone blocks are original Herodian masonry. Millions of handwritten prayers are placed in the cracks between the stones each year.
 | Entities: king-david

**38** · **Caliph Abd al-Malik Builds the Dome of the Rock Over the Foundation Stone** (691)
*Where Muhammad ascended to heaven and Abraham prepared to sacrifice Isaac — sacred to two faiths on one rock*
Built between 685 and 691 CE by Umayyad Caliph Abd al-Malik, the Dome of the Rock is the oldest extant Islamic monument. In Islam, the rock beneath the golden dome is where Prophet Muhammad ascended to heaven during the Night Journey. In Judaism, this is the Foundation Stone where Abraham prepared to sacrifice Isaac and where the Holy of Holies of Solomon's Temple once stood. The octagonal structure with its gilded dome has defined the Jerusalem skyline for over 1,300 years.
 | Entities: abraham

**41** · **Jesus Prays in Agony and Is Betrayed by Judas in the Garden of Gethsemane** (33)
*Where the olive trees may be descendants of those under which Jesus spent his last free hours*
On the night before his crucifixion, Jesus withdrew to the Garden of Gethsemane at the foot of the Mount of Olives to pray. According to the Gospels, he asked God to "let this cup pass from me," sweating drops of blood. It was here that Judas Iscariot identified him with a kiss, leading to his arrest. The garden today contains olive trees estimated at 800–900 years old. The adjacent Church of All Nations, completed in 1924, was built over the remains of a 4th-century Byzantine church.
 | Entities: jesus

**41** · **Christians Establish the Via Dolorosa as the Path Jesus Carried His Cross** (33)
*Where 14 Stations of the Cross mark the 500-meter route through the Old City*
The Via Dolorosa is the processional route believed to trace the path Jesus walked carrying his cross from his condemnation by Pontius Pilate to his crucifixion at Golgotha. The 500-meter route passes through the Muslim and Christian Quarters of the Old City, marked by 14 Stations of the Cross — nine along the street and the final five inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. The current route was established in the 18th century. Franciscan friars have led processions along it every Friday afternoon since the 14th century.
 | Entities: jesus

### Biblical Sites of the Holy Land
**ID**: `holy-land-biblical-sites` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1500 BCE–1st century CE | **Moments**: 7

> From the cave where Jesus was born to the mountain where Moses received the commandments to the fortress where Jewish rebels made their last stand — the physical geography of the Bible.

**83** · **Constantine Builds a Church Over the Cave Where Jesus Was Born** (339)
*Where the oldest site in continuous Christian worship marks the birthplace of Jesus*
The Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem was built over the cave traditionally believed to be the birthplace of Jesus Christ. Commissioned by Emperor Constantine after his mother Helena's visit in 325–326 CE and dedicated in 339 CE, it is the oldest site continuously used as a place of Christian worship. A silver star on the marble floor of the underground grotto marks the exact traditional spot of the birth. The church was rebuilt by Byzantine Emperor Justinian after destruction during the Samaritan revolts.
 | Entities: jesus, mary-mother-jesus

**41** · **Jesus Makes Capernaum His Base for Ministry on the Sea of Galilee** (28)
*Where the ruins of Peter's house and the synagogue where Jesus preached still stand*
The fishing village of Capernaum on the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee served as Jesus's home base during his Galilean ministry. Here he called his first disciples, healed the centurion's servant, and preached in the synagogue. Excavations revealed a 4th-century house church built directly over what tradition identifies as Peter's house, later enclosed by an octagonal Byzantine church. The remains of a 4th-century limestone synagogue, built atop a 1st-century basalt synagogue, are among the most visited ruins in Israel.
 | Entities: jesus

**38** · **Justinian Builds a Monastery at the Foot of the Mountain Where Moses Received the Law** (565)
*Where one of the world's oldest monasteries guards the Burning Bush and ancient manuscripts*
St. Catherine's Monastery sits at the foot of Mount Sinai, where Moses is believed to have received the Ten Commandments. Built by Byzantine Emperor Justinian I between 548 and 565 CE, it is one of the oldest continuously operating Christian monasteries in the world. The monastery contains the world's second-largest collection of early codices and manuscripts after the Vatican, including the Codex Sinaiticus. It also houses what is venerated as the Burning Bush itself. Sacred to Christianity, Islam, and Judaism.
 | Entities: moses

**41** · **John the Baptist Baptizes Jesus in the Jordan River** (29)
*Where the site of the most famous baptism in history was closed by minefields until 2011*
Qasr el-Yahud on the Jordan River is the traditional site where John the Baptist baptized Jesus, marking the beginning of his public ministry. The same location is identified as where the Israelites crossed the Jordan into the Promised Land and where the prophet Elijah ascended to heaven. The site was surrounded by minefields from the 1967 war and remained closed for decades; it was only reopened to visitors in 2011 after a major demining effort.
 | Entities: jesus

**37** · **The Angel Gabriel Appears to Mary in Nazareth** (1969)
*Where the largest church in the Middle East marks the site of the Annunciation*
The Basilica of the Annunciation in Nazareth is built over the grotto where, according to Christian tradition, the Angel Gabriel told the Virgin Mary that she would conceive the Son of God. The current basilica — the largest church in the Middle East — was completed in 1969, the fifth church built on this site. The earliest was a 4th-century synagogue-church, followed by a Crusader-era cathedral. The lower level preserves the venerated grotto and remains of earlier structures.
 | Entities: mary-mother-jesus

**32** · **A Bedouin Shepherd Discovers the Dead Sea Scrolls in a Cave** (1947)
*Where 900 ancient manuscripts including the oldest Hebrew Bible texts were hidden for 2,000 years*
Between 1947 and 1956, over 900 manuscripts dating from the 3rd century BCE to the 1st century CE were discovered in eleven caves near the settlement of Qumran on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea. The scrolls include the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, predating previous manuscripts by a thousand years. They were hidden by what is believed to have been an Essene community. The first scrolls were found by a Bedouin shepherd searching for a lost goat.

**32** · **960 Jewish Rebels Choose Death Over Surrender on a Desert Fortress** (73)
*Where Herod's cliff-top palace became the site of Judaism's most famous last stand*
In 73 or 74 CE, after a three-year siege, the Roman Tenth Legion finally breached the walls of Masada — a cliff-top fortress overlooking the Dead Sea originally built by Herod the Great. According to the historian Josephus, the 960 Jewish Zealots inside chose mass suicide rather than capture. The Romans had constructed a massive assault ramp still visible today. Masada became a symbol of Jewish resistance and determination. Israeli soldiers historically took their oath of service at the summit.

### Artists, Composers, and Cultural Icons
**ID**: `artists-composers-icons` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1791–1980 | **Moments**: 5

> Van Gogh cutting off his ear in Arles, Mozart dying with his Requiem unfinished, Bob Marley playing through tear gas at Zimbabwe's independence, Kurosawa premiering the film that changed cinema, and Pelé scoring his 1,000th goal — the creators and performers who transcended their art.

**80** · **Van Gogh Cuts Off His Ear After a Confrontation with Gauguin in Arles** (1888)
*The painter who sold one painting in his lifetime produces 200 works in 15 months — then mutilates himself*
On December 23, 1888, Vincent van Gogh severed most of his left ear with a razor in the Yellow House in Arles, following an argument with Paul Gauguin. He wrapped the ear and delivered it to a woman at a nearby brothel. During his 15 months in Arles before the incident, Van Gogh had produced roughly 200 paintings in a manic burst of creativity. He sold only one painting in his lifetime and shot himself two years later at age 37.
Kind: event | Entities: vincent-van-gogh

**40** · **Mozart Dies at 35 and Is Buried in an Unmarked Grave in Vienna** (1791)
*The greatest musical prodigy in history dies mid-composition, leaving his Requiem unfinished*
On December 5, 1791, Mozart died in his Vienna apartment at age 35. He had been working feverishly on his Requiem in D minor — commissioned anonymously by a mysterious messenger — when illness overtook him. Despite over 600 compositions including 41 symphonies and iconic operas, Mozart had been struggling financially. He was buried in a common grave at St. Marx Cemetery; the exact location was never marked. The Requiem's commissioner was later revealed to be a count who planned to pass the work off as his own.
Kind: event | Entities: wolfgang-mozart

**38** · **Bob Marley Performs at Zimbabwe's Independence as Tear Gas Fills the Stadium** (1980)
*The voice of Third World liberation plays as a new African nation is born*
On April 17, 1980, Bob Marley and the Wailers performed at Zimbabwe's official independence ceremony at Rufaro Stadium in Harare. During the concert, police fired tear gas at crowds outside trying to get in. Marley stopped playing, the VIP section emptied, but Marley returned to the stage and finished his set while gas hung in the air. He considered Zimbabwe the most important performance of his life. He paid for the band's travel himself. Less than a year later, he was dead from melanoma at age 36.
Kind: event | Entities: bob-marley

**37** · **Akira Kurosawa Premieres Seven Samurai — the Film That Changed Cinema Forever** (1954)
*A three-hour samurai epic from Japan rewrites the grammar of action filmmaking worldwide*
On April 26, 1954, Akira Kurosawa's Seven Samurai premiered in Tokyo. The film — about seven masterless samurai defending a village from bandits — was the most expensive Japanese film ever made, went over budget, and nearly bankrupted Toho Studios. It became the most influential action film in cinema history. George Lucas adapted its plot for Star Wars, John Sturges remade it as The Magnificent Seven, and Kurosawa's techniques became the standard language of Hollywood filmmaking.
Kind: event | Entities: akira-kurosawa

**38** · **Pelé Scores His 1,000th Career Goal at the Maracanã** (1969)
*The greatest footballer in history reaches a milestone no one else has achieved*
On November 19, 1969, Pelé scored his 1,000th career goal on a penalty kick against Vasco da Gama at the Maracanã Stadium in Rio de Janeiro. The game was stopped as fans flooded the pitch. Pelé, in tears, was carried on shoulders. He had scored his first professional goal at 15 and won his first World Cup at 17 — the youngest player ever to do so. He won three World Cups total (1958, 1962, 1970), a feat unmatched. FIFA named him the greatest footballer of the 20th century.
Kind: event | Entities: pele

### Rome's Renaissance Masters
**ID**: `rome-renaissance-masters` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1512–1821 | **Moments**: 4

> The Sistine Chapel ceiling, the painter who died at 37 and was mourned by a Pope, a genius who committed murder, and a poet who died overlooking the Spanish Steps — Rome as the canvas for art's greatest dramas.

**78** · **Michelangelo Unveils the Sistine Chapel Ceiling After Four Years on Scaffolding** (1512)
*A sculptor who insisted he wasn't a painter creates the most famous ceiling in history*
On November 1, 1512, Pope Julius II celebrated Mass beneath the newly completed Sistine Chapel ceiling. Michelangelo had worked for four years (1508-1512), painting 343 figures across 5,000 square feet while lying on his back on scaffolding, paint dripping into his eyes. He wrote a poem about the misery: "My beard toward heaven...my brush, above my face, makes it a splendid floor by dripping down." The result — including The Creation of Adam with its almost-touching fingers — became the most iconic artwork of the Renaissance and possibly of all human civilization.
Kind: event | Entities: michelangelo

**29** · **Raphael Dies at Thirty-Seven and All of Rome Mourns** (1520)
*The painter who rivaled Michelangelo collapses after a fever and is buried in the Pantheon*
On April 6, 1520, Raphael Sanzio died in Rome at the age of 37, reportedly from a fever that developed after excessive physical exertion (Vasari attributed it to romantic excess). His death plunged Rome into mourning. Pope Leo X wept. Raphael's body was laid in state with his final masterpiece, the Transfiguration, displayed at his head. He was buried in the Pantheon at his own request, beneath an inscription that reads: "Here lies Raphael, by whom Nature feared to be outdone while he lived, and when he died, feared she herself would die." He had been painting the Vatican Stanze, rivaling Michelangelo's Sistine ceiling just rooms away.
Kind: event

**34** · **Caravaggio Kills a Man in a Street Brawl and Flees Rome Forever** (1606)
*The most revolutionary painter in Europe becomes a fugitive after a tennis court murder*
On May 29, 1606, Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio killed Ranuccio Tomassoni in a sword fight that erupted during or after a tennis match near the Campo Marzio. The exact cause of the quarrel is debated — gambling debts, a woman, or a political grudge. Caravaggio was wounded and fled Rome with a death sentence on his head. He spent the remaining four years of his life on the run through Naples, Malta, and Sicily, producing some of his greatest works while evading bounty hunters. He died in 1610 at age 38 under mysterious circumstances while trying to secure a papal pardon.
Kind: event | Entities: caravaggio

**28** · **John Keats Dies at Twenty-Five in a Room Overlooking the Spanish Steps** (1821)
*England's greatest Romantic poet succumbs to tuberculosis in a room he asked to be stripped bare*
On February 23, 1821, John Keats died of tuberculosis in a small room at 26 Piazza di Spagna, overlooking the Spanish Steps in Rome. He was 25 years old. In his final months, he asked that the room be stripped of all furnishings to prevent contamination — Italian health laws required everything to be burned after a consumptive's death. His last words to his friend Joseph Severn were: "Don't be frightened — be firm, and thank God it has come." His gravestone, which he dictated himself, reads: "Here lies One whose Name was writ in Water." The room is now the Keats-Shelley Memorial House museum.
Kind: event

### Artists and Writers Who Became Immortal
**ID**: `artists-writers-immortal` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1824–1937 | **Moments**: 6

> A deaf composer who had to be turned around to see his ovation, an author who reinvented Christmas, a painter who turned her pain into self-portraits, and Picasso responding to a bombing with the most famous anti-war painting in history.

**78** · **A Completely Deaf Beethoven Premieres His Ninth Symphony and Has to Be Turned Around to See the Applause** (1824)
*The greatest composer in history conducts his masterpiece without hearing a single note*
On May 7, 1824, Ludwig van Beethoven's Symphony No. 9 in D minor premiered at the Theater am Kärntnertor in Vienna. Beethoven, completely deaf by this point, stood on stage turning pages and gesturing tempo, though the actual conducting was done by Michael Umlauf. When the performance ended, Beethoven was still facing the orchestra and didn't know the audience had erupted. The contralto Caroline Unger walked over and turned him around to see the standing ovation. The audience waved handkerchiefs and hats because they knew he couldn't hear their applause. The Ninth's "Ode to Joy" later became the anthem of the European Union.
Kind: event | Entities: beethoven

**39** · **Frida Kahlo Paints Her Pain into Masterpieces from a Bed in the Blue House** (1929)
*Crippled by a bus accident at eighteen, she turns her suffering into the most personal art in history*
Frida Kahlo was born, lived, and died in La Casa Azul (the Blue House) in Coyoacán, Mexico City. After a devastating bus accident at 18 shattered her spine, pelvis, and right leg, she began painting self-portraits from her bed using a mirror mounted on the canopy. Her 143 paintings — 55 of them self-portraits — depicted her physical agony, turbulent marriage to Diego Rivera, miscarriages, and Mexican identity with unflinching honesty. Dismissed during her lifetime as merely "Diego's wife," she became one of the most recognized and commercially valuable artists of the 20th century decades after her death in 1954.
Kind: event | Entities: frida-kahlo

**40** · **Picasso Paints Guernica in Five Weeks After the Bombing of a Basque Town** (1937)
*The most powerful anti-war painting in history is created in a fury of black and white*
On April 26, 1937, German and Italian bombers destroyed the Basque town of Guernica in Spain during the Civil War, killing hundreds of civilians. Pablo Picasso, living in Paris, was already commissioned to create a painting for the Spanish Pavilion at the Paris World's Fair. Abandoning his original plans, he completed Guernica — a massive 11-by-25-foot painting in black, white, and gray — in just five weeks. It depicted screaming women, a dismembered soldier, a bull, and a horse in agony. The painting became the most famous anti-war artwork in history. When a German officer allegedly asked Picasso "Did you do this?", he reportedly replied: "No, you did."
Kind: event | Entities: pablo-picasso

**39** · **Ernest Hemingway Finishes A Farewell to Arms in a Sweltering Key West Room** (1929)
*A hard-drinking war veteran writes the novel that defines a generation of American literature*
In 1929, Ernest Hemingway completed A Farewell to Arms while living in a second-floor room of a house on Whitehead Street in Key West, Florida. The novel, drawn from his experiences as an ambulance driver in World War I, cemented his reputation as the voice of the Lost Generation. Hemingway would spend the next decade in Key West, writing some of his greatest works including To Have and Have Not and several short stories. The house, with its famous colony of six-toed cats (descendants of a cat given to Hemingway by a ship's captain), is now a museum. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.

Frida Kahlo was born, lived, and died in La Casa Azul (the Blue House) in Coyoacán, Mexico City. *The collision that broke Frida Kahlo's body and started one of the most extraordinary artistic careers in history*
On September 17, 1925, eighteen-year-old Frida Kahlo was riding a wooden bus that collided with an electric streetcar at the intersection of Calzada de Tlalpan and Cuauhtémotzin in Mexico City. A metal handrail pierced her pelvis, fracturing it in three places. Her spine was broken in three places, her collarbone and two ribs were snapped, and her right leg was shattered in eleven fractures. ### Diego Rivera
**ID**: `diego-rivera-murals` | **Type**: biography | **Years**: 1929–1934 | **Moments**: 3

> A 300-pound communist paints the history of Mexico across the national palace, then crosses the border and paints capitalism at its source — when he includes Lenin, Rockefeller destroys the mural.

**64** · **Diego Rivera Paints the Entire History of Mexico Across the Walls of the National Palace** (1929)
*A 300-pound communist transforms the seat of Mexican power into the largest mural cycle in the world*
In 1929, Diego Rivera began painting a massive cycle of murals across the main stairwell and second-floor corridors of the Palacio Nacional in Mexico City. Working over the next 22 years (1929–1951), he covered over 1,500 square meters of wall space with scenes spanning from the Aztec world of Tenochtitlan through the Spanish conquest, colonial rule, independence, and the Mexican Revolution. The central panel depicts the eagle on a cactus — the founding myth of Tenochtitlan — surrounded by scenes of exploitation, resistance, and revolution. Rivera used a technique called true fresco, painting directly onto wet plaster, making the images literally part of the building. The murals remain open to the public for free inside the working seat of Mexico's executive branch.
Kind: event | Entities: diego-rivera

**32** · **Rivera Paints Ford's Assembly Line and Detroit Nearly Destroys the Murals** (1932)
*The world's most famous communist paints capitalism at its most powerful — and the city nearly whitewashes it*
In 1932, Edsel Ford commissioned Diego Rivera to paint murals for the garden court of the Detroit Institute of Arts. Rivera spent months studying the Ford River Rouge plant — the world's largest factory complex — and produced 27 panels depicting the full cycle of automobile production. He included images of workers, machines, raw materials, and scientific research, framing industrial labor as the equal of any subject in classical art. When unveiled in March 1933, religious leaders and editorialists called the murals pornographic and blasphemous, demanding they be whitewashed. Edsel Ford refused, and over 86,000 people visited in the first week. The Detroit Industry Murals are now considered the finest work of public art in America and are a National Historic Landmark.
Kind: event | Entities: diego-rivera

**32** · **Rockefeller Pays Rivera to Paint a Mural, Then Destroys It Because It Contains Lenin** (1934)
*The most notorious act of art censorship in American history happens at 30 Rockefeller Plaza*
In 1933, Nelson Rockefeller commissioned Diego Rivera to paint a 63-foot mural titled "Man at the Crossroads" in the lobby of 30 Rockefeller Plaza in New York City. When Rivera included a portrait of Vladimir Lenin in the composition, Rockefeller asked him to replace it with an anonymous face. Rivera refused. On May 9, 1933, Rivera was paid his full fee of $21,000 and escorted from the building by security. In February 1934, workers smashed the mural to pieces with axes in the middle of the night. Rivera's response was to repaint the work — even larger — in the Palacio de Bellas Artes in Mexico City, this time adding a portrait of John D. Rockefeller Sr. drinking with syphilis bacteria floating nearby. That version survives today.

### Day of the Dead
**ID**: `day-of-the-dead` | **Type**: place | **Years**: 1500 BC–present | **Moments**: 3

> Three thousand years before it became a UNESCO tradition, the Aztecs celebrated a festival of the dead presided over by the goddess Mictecacíhuatl. The Spanish tried to erase it. Instead, it absorbed Catholicism and became the most visually striking ritual in the Americas — families spending the night in cemeteries surrounded by marigold petals and candlelight.

**64** · **The Aztecs Dedicate an Entire Month to the Dead — Presided Over by the Lady of the Underworld** (-1500)
*Mictecacíhuatl, the goddess who swallowed the stars, ruled a festival the Spanish could not kill*
Long before the Spanish arrived, the Aztecs dedicated the ninth month of their calendar — roughly August — to a festival of the dead presided over by Mictecacíhuatl, the Lady of the Dead who ruled the underworld alongside her husband Mictlantecuhtli. Skulls were displayed as trophies, and offerings of food and marigolds were placed for the departed. When the Spanish attempted to replace the tradition with Catholic All Saints' Day in the 16th century, indigenous communities absorbed the new dates (November 1–2) but kept their rituals. The result was a syncretic tradition that survives across Mexico today.
Kind: milestone

**33** · **Families Spend the Entire Night in a Cemetery on an Island Lit by Ten Thousand Candles** (1500)
*On Janitzio Island in Lake Pátzcuaro, the Purépecha keep the most ancient Day of the Dead vigil in Mexico*
On the night of November 1, families on Janitzio Island in Lake Pátzcuaro carry ofrendas — altars laden with marigolds, sugar skulls, pan de muerto, and the favorite foods of the deceased — to the island's small hilltop cemetery. They spend the entire night beside the graves, lighting thousands of candles that turn the cemetery into a glowing beacon visible across the lake. The Purépecha people of Janitzio have maintained this vigil for centuries, making it the oldest continuously practiced Day of the Dead tradition in Mexico. UNESCO inscribed the holiday as Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2008.
Kind: presence

**32** · **A Mexico City Village Illuminates Its Cemetery with Thousands of Candles in the Alumbrada** (1600)
*San Andrés Mixquic draws hundreds of thousands to witness graves blanketed in marigold petals and candlelight*
On the evening of November 2, the village of San Andrés Mixquic in southeast Mexico City performs the Alumbrada — a ceremony in which every grave in the village cemetery is covered in cempasúchil (marigold) petals and lit by dozens of candles. The marigolds form a carpet of orange from the cemetery entrance to each family's graves, creating a path the dead are said to follow home. Mixquic, whose name means "place of the dead" in Nahuatl, was a pre-Columbian settlement built on a lake island. Today it draws hundreds of thousands of visitors on a single night.
Kind: presence

### Sacred Sites of the Eastern Religions
**ID**: `eastern-pilgrimage-sites` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 11th century BCE–present | **Moments**: 5

> The tree where the Buddha found enlightenment, the river that washes away sins, the mountain sacred to four religions that no one has ever climbed, and the temple that changed faiths — the pilgrimage destinations of Asia.

**62** · **Siddhartha Gautama Attains Enlightenment Under the Bodhi Tree** (-500)
*Where the Buddha achieved supreme understanding and Buddhism was born*
Bodh Gaya is the holiest site in Buddhism — the place where Siddhartha Gautama attained supreme enlightenment while meditating under the Bodhi Tree approximately 2,500 years ago, becoming the Buddha. Emperor Ashoka built the first temple here around 250 BCE. The current Mahabodhi Temple dates to the 5th–6th century CE. The Bodhi Tree on the grounds is said to be a direct descendant of the original. Buddhists from across Asia make pilgrimage here, and the site is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

**31** · **Varanasi Becomes the Holiest City in Hinduism — Older Than History Itself** (-1200)
*Where dying brings liberation and the river washes away the sins of a lifetime*
Varanasi is Hinduism's holiest city — one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with settlement dating to at least the 11th century BCE. Hindus believe that bathing in the Ganges here washes away sins, and that dying in Varanasi liberates the soul from the cycle of reincarnation. The city's 88 ghats line the western bank of the river, where the nightly Ganga Aarti fire ceremony at Dashashwamedh Ghat draws thousands. Cremation fires burn around the clock at Manikarnika Ghat.

**31** · **Four Religions Venerate a Mountain That No One Has Ever Climbed** (-1500)
*Where Shiva meditates, the Buddha sits, and no human has ever set foot on the summit*
Mount Kailash at 6,638 meters is sacred to four religions simultaneously — Hinduism (abode of Lord Shiva), Buddhism (seat of Buddha Demchok), Jainism (where the first Tirthankara achieved liberation), and Bon (the seat of all spiritual power). Despite being lower than many climbed Himalayan peaks, no one has ever summited Kailash — China banned all climbing in 2001. The traditional kora circumambulation trek covers 52 kilometers around the base and is undertaken by thousands of pilgrims annually.

**31** · **The Khmer Empire Builds the Largest Religious Monument in the World** (1150)
*Where a Hindu temple became a Buddhist shrine and Cambodia put it on its flag*
Angkor Wat was built by Khmer King Suryavarman II between approximately 1113 and 1150 CE as a Hindu temple dedicated to the god Vishnu. Covering 162 hectares, it is the largest religious monument in the world. Its five towers represent Mount Meru, the cosmic mountain at the center of the Hindu universe. In the late 13th century, the temple was gradually converted to Theravada Buddhism. Its walls contain one of the longest continuous bas-relief narratives in the world. It appears on Cambodia's national flag.

**31** · **Justinian Builds a Cathedral So Grand He Claims to Have Surpassed Solomon** (537)
*Where the world's largest cathedral for 1,000 years became a mosque, then a museum, then a mosque again*
Built by Byzantine Emperor Justinian I between 532 and 537 CE, the Hagia Sophia served as the world's largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years. Its massive dome — 31 meters in diameter — was an engineering marvel. At its consecration, Justinian reportedly exclaimed "Solomon, I have surpassed thee!" After the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in 1453, it became a mosque. Atatürk converted it to a museum in 1934. In 2020, Turkey reconverted it to a mosque, a decision that sparked international controversy.

### Paris: Capital of Culture
**ID**: `paris-capital-culture` | **Type**: era | **Years**: 1874–1925 | **Moments**: 5

> The city where rejected painters invented modern art, a million people followed a writer's coffin, an iron tower became the world's most famous landmark, and a Black woman from Missouri became the highest-paid performer in Europe.

**24** · **Monet, Renoir, and Degas Shock Paris with the First Impressionist Exhibition** (1874)
*Thirty artists rejected by the official Salon display paintings that will redefine art itself*
On April 15, 1874, thirty artists opened an independent exhibition at the studio of photographer Nadar at 35 Boulevard des Capucines. The works by Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Edgar Degas, Camille Pissarro, and Berthe Morisot were savaged by critics. The mocking term "Impressionism" came from a critic's disdain for Monet's Impression, Sunrise. But the exhibition changed the course of art history by proving artists could bypass the gatekeeping Salon system and show directly to the public. Within a decade, Impressionism became the dominant movement in Western art.

**38** · **Two Million Mourners Follow Victor Hugo's Coffin Through Paris to the Panthéon** (1885)
*The author of Les Misérables receives the largest funeral in French history*
On June 1, 1885, an estimated two million people — nearly the entire population of Paris — lined the streets to follow Victor Hugo's coffin from the Arc de Triomphe to the Panthéon. Hugo had requested a pauper's hearse, which created a striking contrast with the massive crowds. He had died on May 22 at age 83, mourned as France's greatest writer and a champion of democracy, abolition, and the poor. His body lay in state beneath the Arc de Triomphe, which was draped in black crepe. The French government converted the Panthéon from a church back into a secular mausoleum specifically to receive his remains.
Kind: event | Entities: victor-hugo

**12** · **Gustave Eiffel Opens His Controversial Iron Tower for the World's Fair** (1889)
*Three hundred artists signed a petition calling it a disgrace — it became the symbol of Paris*
On March 31, 1889, Gustave Eiffel climbed the 1,710 steps to plant a French flag at the top of his newly completed tower, built as the entrance arch to the 1889 World's Fair celebrating the centennial of the French Revolution. At 300 meters, it was the tallest structure in the world — a record it held for 41 years. Before construction, 300 prominent artists and intellectuals, including Guy de Maupassant and Alexandre Dumas fils, signed a petition calling it "a disgrace to Paris." It was originally supposed to be dismantled after 20 years. Over 250 million people have visited it since.
Kind: event

**38** · **Oscar Wilde Dies Penniless in a Paris Hotel Room at Forty-Six** (1900)
*The wittiest man in London ends his life in exile, broken by prison and poverty*
On November 30, 1900, Oscar Wilde died of meningitis in Room 16 of the Hôtel d'Alsace (now L'Hôtel) at 13 Rue des Beaux-Arts. He was 46. After his release from Reading Gaol in 1897, where he served two years of hard labor for "gross indecency" (homosexuality), Wilde had lived in exile in Paris under the name Sebastian Melmoth. He was bankrupt, estranged from his children, and drinking heavily. On his deathbed, he reportedly quipped about the room's wallpaper: "My wallpaper and I are fighting a duel to the death. One of us has to go."
Kind: event | Entities: oscar-wilde

**34** · **Josephine Baker Conquers Paris in a Banana Skirt and Becomes the Highest-Paid Performer in Europe** (1925)
*A Black woman from St. Louis becomes the toast of Jazz Age Paris*
On October 2, 1925, nineteen-year-old Josephine Baker took the stage at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées in La Revue Nègre and electrified Paris. Within months, performing at the Folies Bergère in her iconic banana skirt, she became the highest-paid entertainer in Europe. Baker had fled the racism of America for a France that adored her. During World War II, she served in the French Resistance, smuggling intelligence written in invisible ink on her sheet music. She was awarded the Croix de Guerre and the Legion of Honour. In 2021, she became the first Black woman interred in the Panthéon.

## How to Submit Your Review

Organize your feedback as:

### Section A: Critical Issues
Factual errors, broken content, wrong information. Quote the current text and explain the problem.

### Section B: Tone Issues  
Specific lines that need different tone/framing. Quote current → propose fix.

### Section C: Naming Issues
Moments that fail the five-second test. Current name → proposed rewrite.

### Section D: Structural Suggestions
Category changes, story merges, expansions, reordering.

### Section E: Sacred Content Tone (if Sacred History is in this document)
For each flagged moment: current language → proposed rewrite → where it falls on the devotional↔skeptical spectrum.

### Section F: Narrative Voice & Style
Overall impressions of writing quality, consistency, engagement. What reads well? What falls flat?
