On or around September 28, 551 BCE, Kong Qiu — later known as Confucius — was born in Zou, a village in the state of Lu (modern Qufu, Shandong province). His father Kong He, an elderly garrison commandant, died when Confucius was just three years old, leaving his mother Yan Zhengzai to raise him in poverty. Despite his humble circumstances, Confucius attended schools for commoners and mastered the Six Arts. His ancestry traced to the Shang dynasty nobility, but that lineage offered no material advantage. The contrast between his aristocratic heritage and impoverished upbringing would shape his lifelong obsession with merit over birthright — a radical idea that would eventually restructure Chinese governance for two millennia.

📅 -501 📍 35.5960, 116.9860 general-area major government
Qufu, Shandong Province, China
In 501 BCE, after years of building a reputation as a teacher and thinker, Confucius was appointed governor of a town in Lu, and subsequently rose to the position of Minister of Crime — one of the highest offices in the state. His tenure was immediately controversial: according to the Xunzi, he ordered the execution of Shaozheng Mao, a rival scholar accused of five capital offenses including 'eloquent duplicity' and 'generosity to evildoers.' Confucius then pursued an audacious political project: convincing Lu's three dominant aristocratic families to voluntarily demolish their fortified city walls, stripping them of military independence and restoring authority to the duke. He succeeded with two of the three families through diplomacy alone — a remarkable feat for a man with no army. The experiment collapsed in 498 BCE when the Meng family refused, and Confucius left Lu in 497 BCE, never to hold office again.

In 497 BCE, Confucius left the state of Lu under a carefully managed pretext — the duke had failed to send him his customary portion of sacrificial meat — and began what would become a 14-year journey through the principality states of northeastern and central China. He visited Wey, Song, Zheng, Cao, Chu, Qi, Chen, Cai, and attempted to reach Jin. At each court, he expounded his philosophy of virtuous governance: that rulers should lead by moral example, not force. No prince implemented his ideas. Confucius finally returned to Lu around 484 BCE, after Viscount Ji Huan — his chief political enemy — had died.

Confucius died around 479 BCE in Qufu, aged approximately 71 or 72. In his final years, he had suffered the deaths of his son Kong Li, his most beloved disciple Yan Hui, and another close disciple Zilu — losses he mourned deeply. According to the Analects, he lamented that no ruler had ever truly adopted his principles. Today, an estimated 1.5 billion people live in societies whose legal, familial, and ethical frameworks trace directly to the man who died convinced he had accomplished nothing.

On 19 August AD 14, Augustus died at Nola in Campania, southern Italy, aged 75. He had ruled as Rome's first emperor for 41 years — longer than any subsequent emperor would manage. His body was initially buried in Rome at the Basilica dei Santi Apostoli, but his nephew Leonardo Buonarroti smuggled the corpse to Florence hidden in a bale of merchandise. Michelangelo was reinterred with great ceremony at the Basilica di Santa Croce in Florence on 14 July 1564. His tomb, designed by Giorgio Vasari, stands there today. He had spent 89 years redefining what a single human being could create.

Christopher Columbus was born between 25 August and 31 October 1451 in the Republic of Genoa, the son of Domenico Colombo, a wool weaver, and Susanna Fontanarossa. Growing up on the Ligurian coast, he went to sea by age 14, working as a business agent for wealthy Genoese merchant families. Nothing in his origins predicted that this tradesman's son — largely self-educated — would become the navigator whose 1492 voyage permanently connected two hemispheres of human civilization.

On 3 August 1492, Christopher Columbus departed the Port of Palos de la Frontera, Spain, commanding three ships — the Niña, Pinta, and Santa María — under a commission from Queen Isabella I and King Ferdinand II of Castile and Aragon. San Salvador Island (Guanahani), The Bahamas
On 12 October 1492, after 36 days at sea from the Canary Islands, Columbus's fleet sighted land and made landfall on an island the indigenous Lucayan people called Guanahani, which Columbus renamed San Salvador. This was the first documented European contact with the Caribbean. Columbus encountered the Lucayan people, whom he called 'indios,' assuming he had reached islands off the coast of Asia. He then sailed to Cuba and Hispaniola, establishing a small colony at La Navidad. The date is now recognized as the moment that ended the pre-Columbian era — permanently altering the trajectory of both hemispheres.

In August 1500, royal commissioner Francisco de Bobadilla arrived in Santo Domingo, Hispaniola, to investigate complaints against Columbus's governance. He found Columbus and his brothers presiding over a regime of violent repression — Indigenous Taíno people were enslaved, and Spanish colonists were executed without trial. Though King Ferdinand and Queen Isabella ordered his release upon arrival, they permanently stripped him of his governorship. Christopher Columbus died on 20 May 1506 in Valladolid, Spain, likely from reactive arthritis or heart failure, aged approximately 54. He had spent his final years in legal battles with the Spanish Crown over titles and revenues he believed were owed to him under the 1492 Capitulations of Santa Fe. On 20 July 356 BC, Alexander III was born in Pella, capital of the Kingdom of Macedon, to King Philip II and his wife Olympias. The same day brought Philip news of three events: his general Parmenion had defeated the Illyrian and Paeonian armies, his horses had won at the Olympic Games, and the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus — one of the Seven Wonders of the World — had burned to the ground. In autumn 335 BC, the city of Thebes revolted against Macedonian rule, believing a rumor that Alexander had died in the Balkans. Alexandria, Egypt
In April 331 BC, Alexander founded the city of Alexandria on a narrow strip of land between the Mediterranean Sea and Lake Mareotis in Egypt. According to Plutarch, Alexander personally directed the city's layout, and when no chalk was available to mark the boundaries, he used grain from the soldiers' rations. Seers interpreted the birds that descended to eat the grain as a good omen. Alexandria was designed from the outset as a major port and administrative center. It would become the largest city in the ancient world, home to the Great Library and the Lighthouse — another of the Seven Wonders — and remained the intellectual capital of the Mediterranean for centuries after Alexander's death.

In May 326 BC, Alexander crossed the flooded Hydaspes River (modern Jhelum, Pakistan) at night to surprise the Indian king Porus, who commanded roughly 30,000 troops and 200 war elephants — a weapon Macedonian cavalry had never faced at scale. Alexander's solution was tactical: he used infantry phalanxes to hem in the elephants while his cavalry attacked the flanks. The battle lasted most of a day and was the costliest of his career. On 10 or 11 June 323 BC, Alexander III died in the palace of Nebuchadnezzar II in Babylon, aged 32, after a fever lasting roughly twelve days. He had been planning an invasion of Arabia and intended to make Babylon the capital of his empire. The cause of death remains disputed — typhoid fever complicated by alcohol and exhaustion is the leading modern hypothesis; ancient sources also suggest poisoning. He left no designated heir. Around 1466, Leonardo da Vinci entered the Florence workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio as a garzone (studio boy), becoming a full apprentice by age 17. Verrocchio was the leading painter and sculptor in Florence, and his bottega trained an extraordinary generation including Botticelli, Ghirlandaio, and Perugino. Leonardo spent seven years absorbing drafting, metallurgy, plaster casting, and mechanics alongside painting. According to Giorgio Vasari, when Leonardo painted an angel in Verrocchio's Baptism of Christ (c. 1472–75) with such superior skill, Verrocchio put down his brush and never painted again — a story likely apocryphal, but testament to how quickly the apprentice eclipsed the master.

Florence, Italy
In 1472, Leonardo da Vinci was formally enrolled in the Guild of Saint Luke — Florence's guild of painters and physicians — qualifying him as an independent master at age 20. His father Ser Piero set him up with his own workshop, yet Leonardo continued to live and collaborate with Verrocchio for years afterward. Milan, Italy
In 1482, Lorenzo de' Medici sent Leonardo da Vinci to Milan as an ambassador to Ludovico Sforza, the Duke of Milan. Leonardo brought with him a silver lute shaped like a horse's skull — and a letter. In it, he described ten categories of military engineering he could provide: portable bridges, siege machines, armoured vehicles, cannons, and ships. In 1516, at age 64, Leonardo da Vinci accepted an invitation from King Francis I of France to relocate to the Château du Clos Lucé in Amboise, near the royal court. Leonardo da Vinci died on May 2, 1519, at the Château du Clos Lucé in Amboise, France, aged 67. He bequeathed his notebooks and drawings to his pupil and companion Francesco Melzi, who guarded them carefully but never published them. After Melzi's death, the manuscripts were dispersed across Europe, their contents — detailed anatomical studies, principles of hydrodynamics, designs for flying machines, and observations on geology — largely unknown to the scientific community for generations. Scholars estimate that had Leonardo's findings been published, they could have advanced anatomy by 100 years and engineering by more. His will left his vineyard in Milan to Melzi and his servant Salaì. He was buried at the Collegiate Church of Saint Florentin in Amboise.
